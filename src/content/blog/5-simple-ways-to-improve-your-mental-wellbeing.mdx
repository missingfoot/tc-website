Today kicks off [Mental Health Awareness Week](https://www.mentalhealth.org.uk/campaigns/mental-health-awareness-week), an annual campaign dedicated to breaking down taboos around mental health in our societies by spreading awareness and “inspiring action to promote good mental health for all.” At its core is the understanding that just as we have physical health, we also have mental health.

We are learning about common mental ill health conditions and the things that can cause them – like stress, the focus of this year’s Mental Health Awareness Week. And we are learning how best to support those around us at home and work. But we are still a long way from managing *our own* mental health in the same way we do our physical health.

For many of us, the idea of practicing self-care is a foreign concept. In my workshops, it’s rare to meet an individual who books time into their diary to rest and recharge. Or who can spot helpful and unhelpful coping strategies in dealing with their stress.

It’s no wonder that in any given time [one in six of working age adults have symptoms associated with mental ill health](https://www.lancashirecare.nhs.uk/media/Publications/Mental%20Health%20Fact%20Files/nmhdu-factfile-1.pdf), such as sleep problems or worry. Our lives have become fast-paced. We dash from place to place, connected to the outside world through our technology.

> To sit down and take time for ourselves, or to take the time to talk and listen to another human, can be a rare occurrence.

City living has created a paradox where the buzz of people and connection is set against a huge dose of loneliness and isolation. For those who adopt an urban lifestyle, loneliness will often go hand in hand with stress, with stressors often a combination of financial, work-related, relationships, or physical factors. So it’s hardly surprising that one in three GP work sick notes are now for mental ill health.

What is loneliness and why does it matter? Well, loneliness means people withdraw, feel isolated or have no one to share worries or problems. A [2017 report](http://goodthinkinc.com/hbr-americas-loneliest-workers-according-to-research/?utm_campaign=Mailing%20List&utm_source=hs_email&utm_medium=email&utm_content=62100859&_hsenc=p2ANqtz-9mRFf_2zeABeWc0S2QH4Tf0S4QKjXCTFcrdiKb1WN05MLhHJ1YyMKzWKSU0XmlU7HQcbx87zsgyNlgf29qBoUBocMB6w&_hsmi=62100859) said loneliness was as harmful to health as smoking 15 cigarettes a day. Combined with the fact that this issue is now affecting about 9 million people in the UK, it’s no surprise that we now even have a [Minister for Loneliness](http://www.bbc.co.uk/news/uk-42708507).

We need to be proactive at protecting our mental wellbeing. Taking care of our mental health works the same way as how we should maintain our physical health. For example, if you started to train for a marathon, you wouldn’t go out and run 15 miles on your first training day. There are contributing factors that help you prepare, from the correct running clothing, to the food you fuel your body with, to injury prevention and cross training.

We can apply this approach to taking care of our mental health – with small changes that make a big difference over time.

> When stressed, trying to adopt a mindfulness practice feels like it’s not working. It won’t! At least, not overnight. Maintaining your wellbeing takes time, effort and patience.

Below are my five top tips for small steps you can take to build your own positive mental wellbeing. I suggest exploring each tip, building on the practice daily or weekly, and and finding out what works for you.

### Connect

Spend time with others, and make the effort to meet up and hang out. Sometimes if we have a low mood or are feeling anxious, being around people is one of the least things we want to do. Yet humans are social beings. It is much better when we have talked through how we are feeling, or just have a laugh and forget our worries. Did you know that hugging releases the powerful hormone oxytocin, which regulates social interaction? It is also an antidote to depressive feelings.

### Learn

When was the last time you learnt something new? As we get older, we don’t tend to learn new skills once we leave the education system. Cities always have lots of opportunities to get involved in different events – you just need to seek them out. Take a deep breath and sign yourself up! Anything from cookery class to trapeze school will not only have your brain pumping, but you’ll get to meet new people and form new connections.

### Exercise

Not everyone is a gym bunny, so find your jam and what works for you. Endorphins, the happy hormones, release when we get active. Keeping our bodies healthy supports our mental health to remain positive. It releases all the stress we build up. So whether it is a walk in the park, going out for a run, joining a dance class or playing a team sport, get involved!

### Acts of Kindness

London and any city can be a lonely place. Simple acts like giving up our seat on the tube or holding the door open for a stranger, makes us feel better in ourselves by demonstrating acts of kindness. Other ideas include volunteering, raising money for charity, or getting involved in your workplace community events – all focused on connecting with others. By carrying out acts of kindness, we are directly making a positive impact on someone else’s life – how cool is that?!

### Notice

Notice is about being present in the moment. [Scientific evidence](https://positivepsychologyprogram.com/benefits-of-mindfulness/) demonstrates how regular mindful meditation reduces our blood pressure, gives us more energy, helps us to sleep better and reduces inflammation. You don’t have to be staring cross legged at a wall for hours! Apps like Headspace or Buddify provide short guided meditation to practice daily. It’s the regularity of the practice which is important – not the length. Simple ways to incorporate mindful activities include walking home a different way from work and taking regular breaks to unplug from your smartphone devices. <br />

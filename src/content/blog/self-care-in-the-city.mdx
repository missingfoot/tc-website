The phrase ‘self-care’ has become somewhat of a buzzword in 2018. Generally defined as [the practice of looking after oneself](https://www.myhealth.london.nhs.uk/self-care), millennials in particular have appropriated the idea of self improvement, [spending twice as much](https://www.npr.org/2017/06/04/531051473/the-millennial-obsession-with-self-care) as the baby boomer generation on essentials like workout regimes, therapy, diet plans and apps (which [have grossed](https://techcrunch.com/2018/04/02/self-care-apps-are-booming/) millions of dollars in the first quarter of this year alone.)

When you live in London – [Europe’s biggest metropolis](http://www.bbc.co.uk/news/uk-england-london-31169181) which is also [18 times more crowded than the rest of the UK](http://www.cityam.com/234804/population-explosion-london-is-18-times-more-overcrowded-than-the-rest-of-the-uk-but-its-still-got-nothing-on-dhaka-hyderabad-and-mumbai-) – self-care can be an excellent way of dealing with some of the issues that comes with city dwelling, says cardiology registrar Dr Victoria Robinson. Developing strategies that help you cope with long working hours, city noise and commuting will ultimately benefit your overall well-being.

For Lucy Anna Scott, author of *Mindful Thoughts for City Dwellers*, finding peace means accepting the environment we live in. ‘I think city-dwellers often fall into the trap of believing we can’t look after our mental health or wellbeing in urban environments’ she says. “All too often we assume that only when we’ve ‘escaped’ city life, either on holiday or for good, will we finally find a peaceful life. The truth is that wherever we live, town or country, life has its challenges.”

To relish city life – and feel better in ourselves – we should instead learn to appreciate what it offers. “Cities have another side to them that allow us to take better care of our spiritual health,” Lucy says. “I wanted to explore the more positive flip side of metropolitan life, as well as the simple ways that busy urbanites can disconnect and de-stress.” As adults, we let negative thoughts or anxieties dominate our days, which means we miss so much of what’s good about the city. The key, Lucy explains, is to keep your senses alive to enjoy the city, even as you commute or deal with massive crowds.

It’s easy to start living mindfully in the city. Both Dr Robinson and Lucy advocate introducing greenery into your life, no matter how small. “Nature is the perfect antidote to the concrete of city living and tending to a few herb pots for a few minutes a day can be a soothing ritual for the soul,” says Lucy, while lawyer Simon always heads for somewhere green (usually on his bike) when things start feeling overwhelming.

Being able to find some quiet is particularly important. “Noise pollution is terrible for our blood pressure,” explains Lucy. “So find a place of tranquility – whether a park bench or a gallery – where you can sit in stillness. And being near water is wonderfully calming, as the sound and movement of water helps quieten your mind.”

Even taking a mindful approach to the way you get around the city can be beneficial. Dr Robinson recommends walking as much as possible, so editor Nikki finds her zen by swapping the tube for two legs. “I used to be obsessed with getting places as quickly as possible,” she says. “Now, if I can’t face getting the bus or tube, I’ll walk or run there instead. Even if it takes me longer, at least the journey will be enjoyable.”

And in a city where we’re surrounded by millions of people, it’s important to stay connected. “It’s actually a recipe for a more soulful existence,” says Lucy. “The city has a greater sense of community than we think. It’s often the default to assume other city-dwellers are unfriendly and self-centred, but cities are what they are because of the people that live in them.” Avoid loneliness – a big enemy of mental wellbeing – by interacting through people with similar interests or hobbies. “Connecting with others on a regular basis takes us out of ourselves and our mind away from our own problems,” says Lucy. It’s also why [volunteering is often cited as being good for your health](https://www.wsj.com/articles/research-finds-volunteering-can-be-good-for-your-health-1524449280). As well as fulfilling the mantra of ‘doing good and feeling good’, it means you’re able to concentrate your time on something else.

Helen, a producer, finds her peace by catching up with friends after work – something [that’s recently been scientifically proven to be good for you](http://www.dailymail.co.uk/health/article-5647331/Why-happy-hour-good-Doing-fun-decompress-work-improves-sleep.html), and also something that Dr Robinson suggests scheduling into your diary. “I’ve never regretted meeting a friend for dinner no matter how tired I am,” Helen says. “It frees your mind from work, which is beneficial long term, and it’s always better to see people than not, even if you feel like you need alone time.” But she’s sure to set boundaries. “I firmly say I can’t make something because I’m having a night in to get on top of things – house admin, for example – and stick to it because it will make me feel less stressed in the long term.”

Evaluating your life from a self-care perspective is crucial. “We all need to look after our well being and take responsibility for it,” says Lucy. “Lots of us go to the gym or run to keep our physical bodies fit, but how many of us take our mental fitness seriously? Disconnecting from the daily stresses is a skill and we all need to learn how to do it.”
